## TOPIC 1: ENERGY CONSERVATION ACT 2001

**Q: Discuss the salient features of Energy Conservation Act 2001. (7 marks)**

The Energy Conservation Act was enacted in October 2001 in India. Its primary objective is to reduce the energy intensity of the Indian economy. The government has notified a list of designated consumers in 9 sectors, BEE star labeling, and ECBC norms under this act. 

Key features of the Act are:
1. The Central Government can specify energy consumption standards for appliances and equipment.
2. It mandates standards and labeling for equipment and appliances.
3. Designated consumers (large energy users) must appoint an energy manager, get energy audits done, and comply with norms.
4. It established the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) under the Ministry of Power.
5. It defines penalties for non-compliance with the rules.
6. It introduced the Energy Conservation Building Code (ECBC) for commercial buildings.
7. State Designated Agencies (SDAs) are established for implementation at the state level.
8. The Central Government can prohibit the manufacture or sale of equipment not meeting standards.
9. It sets energy consumption standards for notified equipment.
10. It requires the mandatory display of energy consumption labels on equipment.

## TOPIC 5: CLASSIFICATION OF ENERGY

Renewable Energy comes from sources that are naturally replenished. Examples include solar, wind, hydro, biomass, and geothermal energy.
Non-Renewable Energy comes from sources that get depleted and cannot be replenished in a human timescale. Examples include coal, oil, natural gas, and nuclear energy.
Per Capita Energy Consumption is the total energy consumed by a country divided by its population. India's per capita consumption is about 600 kgoe per year.

**Q: Differentiate between primary and secondary sources and give examples. (4 marks)**

Primary sources are found directly in nature and used without conversion. Examples include coal, crude oil, natural gas, solar radiation, and wind.
Secondary sources are derived from primary sources through a conversion process. Examples include electricity, petrol, diesel, LPG, and coke.

## TOPIC 7: ECBC CODE

**Q: Define ECBC and its significance in promoting energy-efficient building construction. (7 marks)**

ECBC stands for Energy Conservation Building Code. It was launched by BEE in 2007 and updated in 2017 as ECBC 2017. The code applies to commercial buildings with a connected load of 100 kW or more, or a contract demand of 120 kVA or more.

It covers 6 building systems. These are the building envelope, HVAC, lighting, electrical systems, water heating, and renewable energy. It sets minimum energy performance standards for new commercial buildings. There are three levels of compliance: ECBC as the baseline, ECBC+ for enhanced efficiency, and SuperECBC for near net-zero energy buildings. 

ECBC buildings consume 25 to 50 percent less energy than conventional buildings. States have the power to adopt and notify the ECBC with modifications to suit local climatic conditions. It is currently voluntary at the national level, but states like Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, UP, and Rajasthan have made it mandatory.

**Q: Write recommendations for efficient design of furnace. (4 marks)**

1. Complete combustion with minimum excess air. Excess air beyond what is needed carries away heat.
2. Preheating combustion air. Using recuperators to preheat air with flue gas heat saves about 1% fuel for every 20Â°C rise.
3. Preheating the charge. Preheating materials using waste flue gases directly reduces the energy needed to reach operating temperatures.
4. Proper insulation. Using ceramic fiber insulation on furnace walls and doors reduces heat leakage.
5. Controlling furnace draft. Proper draft control prevents air infiltration and incomplete combustion.

**Q: Discuss general fuel economy measures in furnaces. (7 marks)**

1. Complete combustion with minimum excess air: Excess air beyond what is needed for complete combustion carries away heat. Typical excess air should be 10 to 25% depending on fuel type. Monitor O2 in flue gas.
2. Preheating combustion air: For every 20Â°C rise in combustion air temperature, fuel savings of about 1% can be achieved. Use recuperators or regenerators to preheat air using flue gas heat.
3. Preheating the charge: If the charge is preheated using waste flue gases, the furnace needs less energy to bring it to operating temperature. This directly reduces fuel consumption.
4. Controlling furnace draft: Excessive draft increases air infiltration and heat loss. Insufficient draft causes incomplete combustion. Use dampers and draft gauges.
5. Proper insulation: Use ceramic fiber insulation on furnace walls and doors. Every crack or opening leaks heat. Maintain refractory lining regularly.
6. Waste heat recovery from flue gases: Use recuperators, regenerators, or waste heat boilers to recover heat from hot flue gases. Flue gas temperature above 750Â°C has significant recoverable heat.
7. Optimum temperature control: Avoid overheating. Every 10Â°C rise above required temperature increases fuel consumption by about 1 to 2%.
8. Reducing heat stored in furnace structure: Use low thermal mass materials like ceramic fiber instead of firebrick for furnace walls. This reduces heat stored in walls during heating cycles.
9. Minimizing wall losses: Reduce openings, use properly sized doors, and keep doors closed when not loading or unloading.
10. Efficient burner operation: Ensure regular maintenance, proper air to fuel ratio, and clean nozzles.

## Steam Traps

**Q: What are steam traps? State their functions and benefits. (4 marks)**

Definition: A steam trap is an automatic valve that discharges condensate, air, and non-condensable gases from a steam system while preventing the escape of live steam.

Functions:
1. Remove condensate from steam lines and equipment as soon as it forms.
2. Remove air and non-condensable gases from the steam system.
3. Prevent live steam from escaping to conserve steam.

Benefits:
1. Increases heat transfer efficiency because a condensate film on a heat transfer surface acts as an insulator.
2. Prevents water hammer caused by slugs of condensate in steam lines.
3. Reduces corrosion caused by air and CO2 in the steam system.
4. Saves energy by preventing steam loss.
5. Protects equipment from damage due to condensate accumulation.

**Q: List out types of steam traps and explain any two with neat sketches. (7 marks)**

Definition: A steam trap is an automatic valve that discharges condensate, air, and non-condensable gases from a steam system while preventing the escape of live steam.

Functions:
1. Remove condensate from steam lines and equipment as soon as it forms.
2. Remove air and non-condensable gases from the steam system.
3. Prevent live steam from escaping to conserve steam.

Benefits:
1. Increases heat transfer efficiency.
2. Prevents water hammer.
3. Reduces corrosion.
4. Saves energy by preventing steam loss.
5. Protects equipment from damage.

Types of Steam Traps:
1. Mechanical traps work on the density difference between steam and condensate. Examples include the ball float trap and inverted bucket trap.
2. Thermostatic traps work on the temperature difference between steam and condensate. Examples include the balanced pressure trap and bimetallic trap.
3. Thermodynamic traps work on the difference in flow characteristics of steam and condensate. Examples include the disc trap.

Explain Ball Float Trap:
A hollow ball floats on condensate and sinks in steam. When condensate enters the trap body, the float rises and opens the valve, allowing condensate to drain. When steam enters, the float drops and closes the valve, trapping the steam. This trap gives continuous condensate discharge.

## Energy Conservation in Refrigeration and Air Conditioning

**Q: List factors affecting refrigeration and air conditioning system performance and explain any one. Explain techniques of energy conservation in refrigerated cold storage plants. (4 marks)**

Factors affecting system performance:
1. Evaporator temperature and condition. Fouled coils reduce performance.
2. Condenser temperature and condition. A dirty condenser increases head pressure.
3. Refrigerant charge. Undercharge or overcharge both reduce COP.
4. Compressor efficiency and capacity control.
5. Insulation of cold rooms and chilled water pipes.
6. Air infiltration through doors and loading docks.
7. Heat load from lighting, people, and equipment inside the conditioned space.

Explain condenser condition:
If the condenser coils are dirty or fouled, heat rejection is poor. This increases the condensing pressure and temperature. For every 1Â°C increase in condensing temperature, power consumption of the compressor increases by about 3 to 3.5%. Regular cleaning of condenser coils can save 5 to 10% of energy.

**Q: List the energy saving opportunities in refrigeration and air conditioning plant. (7 marks)**

1. Maintain proper condenser cleaning schedule. Dirty condensers increase compressor power by 3% per degree rise in condensing temperature.
2. Increase evaporator temperature. For every 1Â°C increase in evaporator temperature, COP improves by 2 to 3%. Do not cool below the required temperature.
3. Ensure proper refrigerant charge. Both undercharge and overcharge reduce efficiency.
4. Use variable frequency drives (VFDs) on compressors and fans for part-load operation.
5. Reduce air infiltration in cold rooms. Install strip curtains, air curtains, or rapid-closing doors.
6. Improve insulation on cold room walls, floors, and ceilings. Check for damaged insulation.
7. Reduce internal heat loads. Use LED lighting inside cold rooms instead of incandescent. Minimize door opening frequency.
8. Regular maintenance of expansion valves, filters, and oil levels.
9. Use economizer cycle or free cooling when ambient temperature is low enough.
10. Recover condenser waste heat for water heating or space heating.
11. Group similar temperature products together. Avoid mixing products with different temperature requirements.
12. Proper sizing of equipment. Oversized systems cycle on and off frequently, wasting energy.

## Blow Down

**Q: Explain blow down, its necessity, and advantages. (4 marks)**

Blow down is the process of removing a portion of boiler water from the drum to reduce the concentration of dissolved solids, chemicals, and other impurities.

Necessity:
As water evaporates into steam in the boiler, dissolved solids remain behind and their concentration keeps increasing. If not removed, these concentrated impurities cause scale formation on heat transfer surfaces, foaming and carryover of water into steam, and corrosion of boiler tubes. Blow down keeps the total dissolved solids (TDS) within acceptable limits.

Types:
1. Intermittent blow down or bottom blow down. Done manually at intervals. Removes sludge and heavy sediment from the bottom of the boiler drum. Takes about 10 to 15 seconds each time.
2. Continuous blow down. Water is continuously removed from the point of highest TDS concentration. Flow rate is controlled by a valve. Allows heat recovery from blow down water.

Advantages:
1. Prevents scale formation on boiler tubes. Scale even 1mm thick can increase fuel consumption by 5 to 8%.
2. Reduces foaming and carryover of water droplets in steam.
3. Prevents corrosion of boiler tubes and drum.
4. Maintains water quality within prescribed limits.
5. Continuous blow down heat can be recovered using a flash tank and heat exchanger.

## Energy Conservation Opportunities for Boilers

**Q: Discuss the energy conservation opportunities for boiler in detail. (7 marks)**

1. Reduce excess air: Monitor and control excess air using O2 analyzers. For every 1% reduction in excess air, fuel savings of about 0.5% can be achieved. Typical excess air is 15 to 20% for oil and 20 to 30% for coal.
2. Install economizer: Recovers heat from flue gases to preheat boiler feed water. For every 6Â°C increase in feed water temperature, fuel savings of about 1% can be achieved.
3. Install air preheater: Uses flue gas heat to preheat combustion air. For every 20Â°C rise in combustion air temperature, there is about 1% fuel saving.
4. Minimize blow down: Excessive blow down wastes energy. Use automatic TDS controllers. Recover heat from blow down water using flash tanks.
5. Improve boiler insulation: Check and repair damaged insulation on boiler shell, steam pipes, and valves. A bare steam pipe at 200Â°C can lose 1000 kcal/hr per meter length.
6. Recover condensate: Return hot condensate to the boiler feed water tank. Condensate at 90Â°C contains about 50 kcal/kg of heat that would otherwise be wasted.
7. Optimize boiler loading: Operate boiler at 65 to 85% of rated capacity for best efficiency. Very low loads below 25% and overloading both reduce efficiency.
8. Improve feed water treatment: Scale on boiler tubes acts as an insulator. Even 1mm of calcium scale can increase fuel consumption by 5 to 8%. Proper softening and deaeration of feed water prevents scale.
9. Reduce steam leaks: A small leak of 3mm diameter on a 7 kg/cm2 steam line can waste about 30 tons of steam per year.
10. Use waste heat from flue gases: If flue gas exit temperature is above 200Â°C, there is significant scope for heat recovery.
11. Regular soot blowing: Soot deposits on boiler tubes act as an insulator and reduce heat transfer. Regular soot blowing maintains heat transfer efficiency.
12. Monitor and maintain steam traps: Failed steam traps can cause significant steam and condensate losses.

## Losses in Fuel-Fired Furnaces

**Q: Discuss various losses in fuel-fired furnaces in detail. (7 marks)**

1. Heat loss through flue gases or stack loss: This is the largest loss, typically 30 to 40% of total heat input. Hot flue gases carry away sensible heat. Loss depends on flue gas temperature and excess air. Higher flue gas temperature results in higher loss. In a well-designed furnace, this should be minimized to 20 to 25%.
2. Heat loss due to incomplete combustion: If combustion is incomplete, CO is formed instead of CO2. CO has a calorific value of 2400 kcal/Nm3 that is wasted. This is caused by insufficient air, poor mixing, or low combustion temperature.
3. Heat loss through furnace walls or wall loss: Heat is conducted through furnace walls and radiated from outer surfaces. This depends on insulation thickness, wall temperature, and furnace surface area. Typically 2 to 5% of total input.
4. Heat loss due to opening in furnace: Every time the furnace door opens, hot gases escape and cold air enters. Radiation heat loss through openings is proportional to the fourth power of temperature. Keep doors closed and properly sealed.
5. Heat stored in furnace structure: During heating, the furnace walls, hearth, and roof absorb heat. This is a loss during batch operations because stored heat is wasted during the cooling period. Use low thermal mass materials like ceramic fiber to reduce this loss.
6. Heat loss due to water cooling: In furnaces with water-cooled components like doors, frames, and skid pipes, heat is removed by cooling water. This heat is usually not recovered.
7. Heat loss due to scale formation: Oxidation of the metal surface forms scale. Scale formation represents material loss and the heat absorbed by the oxidation process.
8. Heat carried away by the heated material or useful heat: This is not actually a loss, it is the useful output. However, if the product is overheated beyond requirement, the extra heat is wasted.

## Feed Water Treatment and Deaeration

**Q: Explain deaeration treatment for boiler water. (3 marks)**

Deaeration is the process of removing dissolved gases, mainly oxygen and carbon dioxide, from boiler feed water before it enters the boiler.

Why it is needed:
Dissolved oxygen causes pitting corrosion on boiler tubes and drum. CO2 forms carbonic acid which causes acidic corrosion. Even small amounts like 0.1 ppm of O2 can cause significant damage over time.

How it works:
Feed water is heated to its saturation temperature in a deaerator vessel. At saturation temperature, the solubility of gases in water drops to near zero, and dissolved gases are released. These gases are vented out through a vent condenser at the top of the deaerator. The deaerator also acts as a feed water heater, raising the water temperature to 105 to 110Â°C.

Types:
1. Spray type deaerator.
2. Tray type deaerator.

## TOPIC 1: CLEAN DEVELOPMENT MECHANISM (CDM)

**Q: What is CDM? Write a note on CDM methodology and its project cycle with neat sketch. Explain the objectives of CDM and how CDM promotes sustainable development. (7 marks)**

CDM is one of the three flexible mechanisms defined in the Kyoto Protocol under Article 12. It allows developed countries to invest in emission reducing projects in developing countries and earn Certified Emission Reductions (CERs) or carbon credits. Each CER equals one tonne of CO2 equivalent not emitted.

Objectives:
1. Help developing countries achieve sustainable development.
2. Help developed countries meet their emission reduction targets cost effectively.
3. Transfer clean technology and financial resources to developing countries.

CDM Project Cycle:
1. Project Design: The project developer prepares a Project Design Document (PDD) describing the project activity, baseline methodology, monitoring plan, and expected emission reductions.
2. National Approval: The project must be approved by the Designated National Authority (DNA) of the host country. In India, this is the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change. DNA confirms the project contributes to sustainable development.
3. Validation: An independent Designated Operational Entity (DOE) accredited by the CDM Executive Board reviews the PDD and validates whether it meets CDM requirements.
4. Registration: The DOE submits the validated PDD to the CDM Executive Board for formal registration. Registration is the official acceptance of the project as a CDM project.
5. Monitoring: The project developer monitors the emission reductions as per the monitoring plan in the PDD. Monitoring data is collected throughout the crediting period.
6. Verification: A second independent DOE verifies the monitored emission reductions. This DOE must be different from the one that did validation.
7. Issuance of CERs: After successful verification, the CDM Executive Board issues CERs for the verified emission reductions. These CERs can be traded in the carbon market.

## TOPIC 2: BACHAT LAMP YOJNA (BLY)

**Q: How does the Bachat Lamp Yojna work? (3 marks)**

Bachat Lamp Yojna (BLY) was launched by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) in 2009. It is a CDM based scheme for distributing CFL and later LED bulbs to households across India at subsidized rates. Under this scheme, BEE partners with private companies called Implementing Entities. These companies distribute CFL or LED bulbs to households by collecting old incandescent bulbs. The company recovers its cost through the sale of CERs generated by the energy savings from replacing inefficient bulbs. Households get efficient bulbs at a price of Rs 15 instead of Rs 80 to 100.

**Q: Explain Bachat Lamp Yojana, its aim and benefits. (7 marks)**

Aim: The aim is to reduce electricity consumption of the domestic lighting sector by replacing inefficient incandescent bulbs with energy efficient CFL or LED bulbs while using the CDM carbon credit mechanism to fund the subsidy.

Scheme Design:
1. BEE acts as the coordinating agency.
2. Private Implementing Entities (IEs) are selected through competitive bidding.
3. IEs distribute CFLs or LEDs to households at highly subsidized prices.
4. Old incandescent bulbs are collected and destroyed to prevent reuse.
5. The energy savings from replacing bulbs qualify as a CDM project.
6. IEs register the project with UNFCCC and earn CERs.
7. Revenue from selling CERs in the international carbon market covers the subsidy cost.
8. Each CFL replacing a 60W incandescent bulb saves about 45W. Operating 4 hours per day saves 65 kWh per year.

Benefits:
1. Reduces household electricity bills by 40 to 50 percent on lighting.
2. Reduces peak electricity demand since lighting accounts for 20 percent of India's peak demand.
3. Reduces CO2 emissions as each CFL saves about 40 kg CO2 per year.
4. Costs the government nothing since the scheme is self financing through CERs.
5. Generates employment in distribution and monitoring.
6. Promotes awareness about energy efficiency in households.
7. Reduces the need for building new power plants to meet lighting demand.

The scheme originally targeted distributing 400 million CFLs across India. Later, the focus shifted from CFLs to LED bulbs under the UJALA scheme launched in 2015.

## TOPIC 3: UNFCCC

**Q: Discuss the significance of UNFCCC in addressing global climate change challenges. (4 marks)**

UNFCCC stands for United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. It is an international environmental treaty adopted at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil in June 1992. It came into force on 21 March 1994. Currently, 198 countries have ratified the Convention. The main objective of UNFCCC is to stabilize greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that prevents dangerous human interference with the climate system. **Q: Write a short note on climate change and development of UNFCCC. (7 marks)**

UNFCCC stands for United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. It is an international environmental treaty adopted at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil in June 1992. It came into force on 21 March 1994. The main objective of UNFCCC is to stabilize greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that prevents dangerous human interference with the climate system.

Key principles of UNFCCC:
1. Common but differentiated responsibilities: Developed countries should lead in reducing emissions.
2. Precautionary principle: Lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason to delay action.
3. Right to sustainable development: Countries have the right to promote sustainable development.
4. Supportive and open international economic system: Trade measures should not be used as barriers.

Classification of countries:
1. Annex I: Developed countries and economies in transition including USA, EU, Japan, Russia, and Australia.
2. Annex II: Subset of Annex I wealthy developed countries required to provide financial and technical support to developing countries.
3. Non-Annex I: Developing countries including India, China, and Brazil.

Major outcomes and agreements under UNFCCC:
1. Kyoto Protocol (1997): First legally binding emission reduction targets for developed countries.
2. Copenhagen Accord (2009): Voluntary pledges.
3. Paris Agreement (2015): All countries agreed to limit warming to 2Â°C with Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs).

UNFCCC operates through annual meetings called Conference of Parties (COP). The COP is the supreme decision making body of the Convention. UNFCCC created the framework for all subsequent climate agreements. Without UNFCCC, there would be no Kyoto Protocol, no Paris Agreement, and no CDM mechanism.

## TOPIC 4: KYOTO PROTOCOL

**Q: Explain the principles and objectives of the Kyoto Protocol. Assess the effectiveness of the Kyoto Protocol in reducing greenhouse gas emissions. (4 marks)**

The Kyoto Protocol is an international agreement linked to UNFCCC that was adopted in Kyoto, Japan on 11 December 1997 and came into force on 16 February 2005.

Key features:
1. Set legally binding emission reduction targets for developed countries. First commitment period was 2008 to 2012 to reduce GHG emissions by an average of 5.2 percent below 1990 levels. Second commitment period was 2013 to 2020 via Doha Amendment.
2. Covers six greenhouse gases: CO2, CH4, N2O, HFCs, PFCs, SF6.
3. Has three flexible mechanisms to help countries meet targets: CDM, Joint Implementation, and Emissions Trading.
4. Developing countries including India and China have no binding targets.
5. USA never ratified the Protocol and Canada withdrew in 2012.

Effectiveness: The Protocol succeeded in reducing emissions in many developed countries. The EU met their collective target. However, global emissions continued to rise because major emitters like USA, China, and India were not bound by targets. This led to the Paris Agreement in 2015 which covers all countries.

**Q: Explain Sustainable development. (3 marks)**

Sustainable development is defined as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It rests on three pillars: economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental protection. In the context of energy, sustainable development means using energy resources efficiently, switching to renewable sources, and reducing pollution and greenhouse gas emissions while maintaining economic growth.

## TOPIC 5: PROTOTYPE CARBON FUND (PCF)

**Q: What is the Prototype Carbon Fund? What are its objectives? State the three primary strategic objectives of PCF. (3 marks)**

The Prototype Carbon Fund (PCF) was launched by the World Bank in April 2000 with capital of US$ 180 million contributed by 6 governments and 17 private companies. PCF was the first carbon fund in the world. It was designed to demonstrate how project based greenhouse gas emission reductions under the Kyoto Protocol CDM and JI mechanisms could work in practice.

Three primary objectives of PCF:
1. To show how project based transactions for greenhouse gas emission reductions can contribute to sustainable development and lower the cost of compliance with Kyoto Protocol targets.
2. To provide a learning experience for stakeholders on how the CDM and JI mechanisms work in practice.
3. To demonstrate how the World Bank can work as a financial intermediary in the carbon market, acting as trustee of the fund and purchasing emission reductions on behalf of contributors.

PCF invested in 23 projects in developing countries and economies in transition covering renewable energy, energy efficiency, and waste management.

## TOPIC 6: CONFERENCE OF PARTIES (COP)

**Q: State the roles and responsibilities of COP (Conference of the Parties). (4 marks)**

COP stands for Conference of the Parties. It is the supreme decision making body of the UNFCCC. All countries that have ratified the Convention are represented at COP. COP meets annually in different cities around the world.

Roles and responsibilities:
1. Review the implementation of the Convention and its related legal instruments.
2. Make decisions necessary to promote effective implementation of the Convention.
3. Adopt new protocols, amendments, or annexes to the Convention.
4. Review national communications and emission inventories submitted by Parties.
5. Assess the overall effects of measures taken by Parties to address climate change.
6. Mobilize financial resources for climate action in developing countries.
7. Review reports from subsidiary bodies.

Notable COPs include COP 3 in Kyoto for the Kyoto Protocol, COP 15 in Copenhagen for the Copenhagen Accord, COP 21 in Paris for the Paris Agreement, and COP 26 in Glasgow for the Glasgow Climate Pact.

## TOPIC 7: CARBON CREDIT TRADE SCHEME

**Q: Explain the Carbon Credit Trade Scheme. (3-4 marks)**

A carbon credit is a tradable permit or certificate that allows the holder to emit one tonne of CO2 or an equivalent amount of another greenhouse gas.

How the scheme works:
1. A regulatory body sets a cap on total emissions allowed.
2. Emission allowances are distributed or auctioned to industries.
3. Companies that reduce their emissions below their allowance have surplus credits.
4. Companies that emit more than their allowance must buy credits from those with surplus.
5. This creates a market based mechanism where the price of carbon is determined by supply and demand.
6. The system rewards companies that invest in clean technology and penalizes those that continue polluting.

Types of carbon markets include compliance markets created by mandatory regulations and voluntary markets where companies voluntarily buy carbon credits to offset their emissions.

India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) was notified in June 2023 under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022. BEE is the administrator. It covers obligated entities in designated sectors. India plans to have a fully operational carbon market by 2026.

## TOPIC 8: PERFORM ACHIEVE AND TRADE (PAT) SCHEME

**Q: Explain the Perform Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme. (3-4 marks)**

PAT is a market based energy efficiency trading mechanism under the National Mission for Enhanced Energy Efficiency. This is one of the eight missions under India's National Action Plan on Climate Change.

How PAT works:
1. BEE identifies Designated Consumers (DCs) in energy intensive sectors such as thermal power plants, iron and steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, textile, pulp and paper, chlor alkali, railways, refineries, petrochemicals, distribution companies, and buildings.
2. Each DC is given a Specific Energy Consumption (SEC) reduction target based on their current performance.
3. DCs that reduce their SEC beyond the target earn Energy Saving Certificates (ESCerts).
4. DCs that fail to meet their target must buy ESCerts from the market or pay a penalty.
5. ESCerts are traded on two power exchanges: Indian Energy Exchange and Power Exchange India Limited.

PAT Cycle I covered 478 designated consumers and achieved energy savings of 8.67 million tonnes of oil equivalent against a target of 6.686 MTOE. This exceeded the target by about 30 percent. PAT is currently in its 7th cycle, covering over 1000 designated consumers across 13 sectors.

For running Stable Diffusion 3.5 and Flux.1 locally in 2026, VRAM requirements depend heavily on the model variant and the use of quantization (such as FP8, GGUF, or NF4)[1][2]. 

### **Stable Diffusion 3.5 VRAM Requirements**
*   **SD 3.5 Medium:** Designed to be more accessible, it requires approximately **9.9 GB of VRAM** for full performance (FP16)[3]. With GGUF quantization, it can run on GPUs with less VRAM[4].
*   **SD 3.5 Large:** This is a much heavier model (8B parameters)[5]. While it can technically run on lower-VRAM cards using techniques like offloading or quantization, it generally requires **~18 GB of VRAM** for standard FP16 inference[6][7]. Users on cards with 16 GB or less may experience significantly slower performance if the system resorts to offloading to RAM[8][9].

### **Flux.1 VRAM Requirements**
Flux.1 is more memory-intensive than previous Stable Diffusion models due to its DiT (Diffusion Transformer) architecture[10].
*   **Full Quality (FP16):** Requires **18–24 GB of VRAM**[1][10]. An RTX 4090 (24 GB) is widely considered the gold standard for running these models without compromise[10].
*   **Quantized (FP8/NF4/GGUF):** 
    *   **FP8:** Typically requires **13–15 GB**[1][2].
    *   **NF4/GGUF (Q4):** Can run on as little as **6–10 GB of VRAM**, making them accessible to common mid-range cards like the RTX 3060 (12 GB) or 4060 (8 GB), though generation speed and quality will vary based on the level of compression[1][11][10].

### **Recommendations for 2026**
*   **The Practical Floor:** For a modern workflow that includes ControlNet, LoRAs, and higher resolutions, **12 GB to 16 GB of VRAM** is the recommended minimum[12][13].
*   **Professional/High-Res Work:** **24 GB or more** is strongly recommended to handle heavy extension stacks, batch generation, and full-precision model inference without needing to rely on slow CPU offloading[12][14][13].
*   **Workflow Tip:** Using **ComfyUI** is highly recommended as it handles model offloading dynamically and supports GGUF/quantized formats that allow these large models to run on consumer-grade hardware that would otherwise be insufficient[5][4][2].

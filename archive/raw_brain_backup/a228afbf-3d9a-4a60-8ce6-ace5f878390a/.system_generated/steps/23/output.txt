7. Chest X-Ray (CXR)
Definition
A chest radiograph is a non-invasive projection transmission image displaying the differential radiodensity of thoracic tissues: air (black/radiolucent), fat (dark gray), water/soft tissue (light gray), and bone/metal (white/radiopaque).
Standard Projections
Posteroanterior (PA) View: Gold standard; taken in full upright inspiration with X-ray beam travelling from back to front; minimizes cardiac magnification.
Anteroposterior (AP) View: Performed at bedside on immobilized or ICU patients; magnifies cardiac silhouette size and limits scapular clearance.
Systematic Evaluation Approach (ABCDE Method)
A - Airway: Trachea position (normally midline, vertically oriented over spinous processes).
B - Breathing / Lung Fields: Vascular markings, lucency, symmetric expansion, pleural spaces.
C - Cardiac Silhouette & Mediastinum: Cardiothoracic Ratio (CTR) on PA view is normally less than 0.50 (50%); mediastinum shape and borders.
D - Diaphragm: Both hemidiaphragms rounded, smooth, right sitting 1 to 2 cm higher than left; costophrenic and cardiophrenic angles sharp and clear.
E - Everything Else / Skeleton: Ribs, clavicles, humeri, subcutaneous tissues, indwelling tubes, catheters, pacing wires.
Common Pathological Variations on CXR
Lobular Consolidation (Pneumonia):
Homogeneous radio-opacity with preservation of lung volume.
Presence of "Air Bronchograms" (branching lucent tubular airways outlined by fluid-filled alveoli).
Silhouette sign: Loss of the right heart border indicates right middle lobe consolidation.
Pleural Effusion:
Blunting of the costophrenic angle (evident when fluid exceeds 200 mL).
Meniscus sign: Homogeneous dense opacity with a concave superior border sloping upward toward the lateral chest wall.
Massive effusion displaces the mediastinum and trachea away from the affected side.
Pneumothorax:
Complete absence of lung markings at the peripheral thoracic cavity.
Visible sharp white visceral pleural line separated from the chest wall by radiolucent air.
Tension Pneumothorax: Marked tracheal and mediastinal shift toward the contralateral side with flattened/depressed ipsilateral hemidiaphragm.
Atelectasis (Lung Collapse):
Opacity accompanied by direct signs of volume loss: tracheal, cardiac, and mediastinal shift toward the side of collapse.
Crowding of ribs and elevation of the ipsilateral hemidiaphragm.
COPD / Emphysema:
Bilateral pulmonary hyperinflation with increased lung field radiolucency.
Flattened, depressed hemidiaphragms with widened intercostal spaces.
Elongated, narrow "tubular" heart shadow; enlarged retrosternal clear space on lateral view.
Pulmonary Edema (Congestive Heart Failure):
Cardiomegaly (CTR greater than 0.50).
Cephalization of pulmonary vessels (upper lobe blood diversion).
Perihilar "Bat-wing" or "Butterfly" alveolar infiltrates.
Kerley B lines (short, horizontal peripheral lines representing thickened interlobular septa).
Physiotherapy Implications
Localizes pulmonary pathologies to specific segments, directly guiding postural drainage positioning.
Consolidation/Atelectasis confirms the need for thoracic expansion techniques, chest percussion, vibration, and directed autogenic drainage.
Pneumothorax is a strict contraindication to positive pressure techniques (PEP, IPPB) and vigorous manual chest clapping until an intercostal chest tube (ICD) is placed and underwater seal bubbling stabilizes.
8. Tests for Peripheral Arterial and Venous Conditions
Definition
Clinical and non-invasive diagnostic tests evaluating arterial flow velocity, lumen patency, tissue perfusion, and venous valvular competence in the lower and upper extremities.
Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD) Tests
Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI):
Definition: Ratio of the highest systolic blood pressure at the ankle to the highest systolic blood pressure in the brachial arteries.
Procedure: Patient rests supine for 10 minutes. A Doppler ultrasound probe (5 to 10 MHz) and pneumatic cuff measure systolic blood pressure in both brachial arteries and bilateral ankle arteries (Dorsalis Pedis and Posterior Tibial).
Calculation: \text{ABI} = \frac{\text{Highest Ankle Systolic Pressure (DP or PT)}}{\text{Highest Brachial Systolic Pressure (Right or Left)}}
Clinical Values and Interpretation:
1.00 to 1.40: Normal arterial perfusion.
0.91 to 0.99: Borderline arterial disease.
0.41 to 0.90: Mild-to-moderate PAD (characteristic intermittent claudication during walking).
Less than or equal to 0.40: Severe PAD (critical limb ischemia, rest pain, gangrene risk).
Greater than 1.40: Non-compressible, calcified rigid arteries (commonly seen in diabetes mellitus; requires Toe-Brachial Index).
Buerger�s Test (Postural Color Changes):
The patient lies supine; the examiner elevates the lower extremity to 45 to 60 degrees for 1 to 2 minutes.
Abnormal: Development of marked pallor in less than 60 seconds (positive vascular angle of Buerger less than 20 degrees indicates severe ischemia).
On returning the leg to a dependent position, a delay in pink reperfusion greater than 15 seconds followed by a dark purplish-red discoloration (Rubor of Dependency) confirms chronic arterial insufficiency.
Capillary Refill Time (CRT):
Firm pressure applied to the distal digital pulp/nail bed for 5 seconds until blanched.
Normal: Color returns in less than 2 seconds; prolonged CRT indicates distal hypoperfusion.
Peripheral Venous Disease Tests
Wells Clinical Score for Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT):
Stratifies pre-test probability of DVT based on risk factors: active cancer (+1), paralysis/recent plaster immobilization (+1), bedridden over 3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks (+1), localized tenderness along deep venous system (+1), entire leg swollen (+1), calf swelling greater than 3 cm compared to asymptomatic side (+1), pitting edema confined to symptomatic leg (+1), collateral superficial non-varicose veins (+1), alternative diagnosis as likely as DVT (-2).
Score greater than or equal to 2: DVT likely (urgent Duplex Ultrasound required).
Homan�s Sign:
Passive dorsiflexion of the ankle with the knee extended to elicit calf pain.
Clinical Note: Insensitive, non-specific, and carries a mechanical risk of dislodging an unstable clot. It is no longer recommended in modern clinical guidelines.
Trendelenburg Test (Brodie-Trendelenburg Test):
Differentiates saphenofemoral junction incompetence from deep/perforator valvular incompetence in varicose veins.
Leg is elevated to empty superficial veins; a tourniquet is applied around the upper thigh.
Patient stands: rapid retrograde filling from above before releasing the tourniquet indicates perforator incompetence; rapid filling from above after tourniquet release confirms saphenofemoral junction valve incompetence.
Venous Duplex Ultrasound:
Gold standard non-invasive diagnostic investigation for DVT; evaluates venous compressibility and venous flow patterns.
Physiotherapy Implications and Precautions
In Intermittent Claudication (PAD with ABI 0.5 to 0.9): Supervised treadmill walking is the primary conservative therapy. Exercise to moderate-to-strong claudication pain (grade 3 on 4-point scale), rest until pain resolves, and repeat for 30 to 45 minutes, 3 to 5 times weekly.
In Severe PAD (ABI less than 0.5): High-intensity exercise, leg elevation, and external compression bandages are strictly contraindicated.
In Acute Confirmed DVT: Hold all ambulation, range of motion exercises, and calf massage until adequate therapeutic anticoagulation is established (typically 24 to 48 hours with Low Molecular Weight Heparin).
In Chronic Venous Insufficiency: Promote leg elevation above heart level, prescribe graduated compression stockings (20 to 30 mmHg), and prescribe active calf muscle pump exercises./////////

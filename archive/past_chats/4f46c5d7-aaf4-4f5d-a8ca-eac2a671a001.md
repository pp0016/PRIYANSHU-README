Instead of Electrical Resistance ($R$), we have **Thermal Resistance ($R_{th}$)**.

**Thermal Resistance ($R_{th}$) = $\frac{L}{kA}$**

### 2. What is he doing on the board?

This is asked in 5 out of 9 past papers (W24, W23, S26, S25, S24). GTU usually asks: *"Explain the physical significance of critical radius of insulation and derive an expression for the same in case of a cylinder."*

Here is exactly what you need to write in your exam:

### 1. Physical Significance (2 Marks)
If you add insulation to a flat wall, heat transfer always decreases. But if you add insulation to a curved surface (like a pipe/cylinder), heat transfer initially **increases** up to a certain radius, and only starts decreasing after that. 
The radius at which heat transfer is **maximum** is called the Critical Radius of Insulation ($r_c$). 
*   If $r < r_c$: Adding insulation increases heat loss (bad for pipes, good for electrical wires).
*   If $r > r_c$: Adding insulation decreases heat loss (good for steam pipes).

### 2. The Derivation for a Cylinder (5 Marks)
**Step 1: Write the total thermal resistance equation**
For a pipe with insulation, there is conduction through the insulation and convection to the outside air:
$$ R_{total} = \frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2 \pi k L} + \frac{1}{h(2 \pi r_2 L)} $$
*(Where $r_1$ is inner radius, $r_2$ is outer radius, $k$ is conductivity of insulation, $h$ is outside convection).*

**Step 2: Write the Heat Transfer equation**
$$ Q = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{total}} $$
To maximize $Q$, we must **minimize** $R_{total}$. 

**Step 3: Differentiate Resistance with respect to $r_2$ and set to Zero**
In calculus, to find the minimum of a function, you differentiate it and equal it to 0.
$$ \frac{d(R_{total})}{d(r_2)} = 0 $$

**Step 4: Do the math**
$$ \frac{d}{d(r_2)} \left[ \frac{\ln(r_2) - \ln(r_1)}{2 \pi k L} + \frac{1}{h(2 \pi r_2 L)} \right] = 0 $$

$$ \frac{1}{2 \pi k L} \left( \frac{1}{r_2} \right) + \frac{1}{h(2 \pi L)} \left( \frac{-1}{r_2^2} \right) = 0 $$

**Step 5: Solve for $r_2$ (which becomes $r_c$)**
Cancel out the common $2 \pi L$:
$$ \frac{1}{k \cdot r_2} - \frac{1}{h \cdot r_2^2} = 0 $$
$$ \frac{1}{k \cdot r_2} = \frac{1}{h \cdot r_2^2} $$
Cross multiply:
$$ h \cdot r_2^2 = k \cdot r_2 $$
Divide by $r_2$:
$$ r_2 = \frac{k}{h} $$

**Final Answer:**
$$ r_c = \frac{k}{h} $$

### 🤖 Antigravity
Next up is a massive GTU favorite: **Critical Radius of Insulation**. 
*(Asked in 5 out of 9 papers, usually for 7 marks).*

Here is everything you need to know, without the fluff.

### 1. The Core Concept (Why do we care?)
Normally, you think adding insulation *stops* heat loss. But for curved surfaces (pipes and wires), **adding insulation at first actually INCREASES heat loss.**
*   **Why?** Because as you add insulation, the outside surface area gets bigger. A bigger surface area means more heat is lost to the air via convection. 
*   Until you reach a specific thickness (the **Critical Radius**, $r_c$), the extra surface area loses heat faster than the insulation can trap it.
*   **Rule:** If your pipe is smaller than $r_c$, adding insulation cools it down faster (good for electrical wires). If your pipe is bigger than $r_c$, adding insulation traps heat (good for steam pipes).

### 2. The Two Formulas You Must Memorize
1.  **For a Cylinder (Pipe/Wire):** $$r_c = \frac{k}{h}$$
2.  **For a Sphere:** $$r_c = \frac{2k}{h}$$
*(Where $k$ is the thermal conductivity of the insulation, and $h$ is the outside convection coefficient).*

### 3. The Derivation for a Cylinder (The 7-Mark Question)
GTU frequently asks: *"Explain physical significance of critical radius of insulation and derive an expression for the same in case of cylinder."* 

Here is exactly what you write for the derivation:

**Step 1: Write the Total Thermal Resistance ($R_{total}$) for an insulated pipe**
$$ R_{total} = R_{conduction} + R_{convection} $$
$$ R_{total} = \frac{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}{2 \pi k L} + \frac{1}{h(2 \pi r_2 L)} $$
